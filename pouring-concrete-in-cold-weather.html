Pouring Concrete in Cold Weather: Temps, Heaters & Curing

Can you pour concrete in cold weather? Learn the temperature limits, when to use heaters and blankets, how to protect a fresh slab, and when to wait.

Updated October 15, 2026. General guidance only. Follow your local code, your concrete supplier and a professional for structural work.

Short answer: yes, you can pour concrete in cold weather, but the plan has to change. Cold slows the chemical reaction that makes concrete harden, and ice in fresh concrete can permanently damage it. Most problems come from freezing in the first day or two, pouring on frozen ground, and covering the slab too late.

Why cold weather is a problem

Concrete hardens by hydration, a reaction between cement and water. Cooler temperatures slow it down, so the slab stays soft and finishable for longer and gains strength more slowly. The bigger danger is freezing. If the water in fresh concrete freezes before the concrete has gained enough strength (a commonly cited figure is about 500 psi), the surface can turn weak and crumbly and the slab may never reach its design strength.

What counts as cold weather?

Industry guidance (ACI 306) treats a period as cold weather when the average daily air temperature falls below about 40°F (5°C) for several days in a row, and the temperature stays at or below 50°F (10°C) for more than half of each day. In practice, start planning for cold-weather methods whenever the forecast is near or below 40°F during the pour and the days after.

Before the pour: checklist

  • Check the ground. Never pour on frozen subgrade or snow. Thaw it first, for example with insulated blankets laid the day before.
  • Clear the forms. Remove snow and ice from forms and rebar.
  • Order the right mix. Tell the plant it is a cold-weather pour. Many suppliers can use warm water and non-chloride accelerators. Ask what they recommend for your temperature.
  • Schedule it early in the day. A morning pour gives the slab more warm hours to gain strength before the night.
  • Have materials ready. Blankets, plastic sheeting and, if needed, a heater should be on site before the truck arrives.

Heaters: what to know before you rent one

Construction heaters are often rented for winter pours. Two things matter most:

  • Use vented (indirect-fired) heaters in enclosed areas. Unvented heaters release carbon dioxide, which can react with the fresh surface and leave it soft and dusty. Keep exhaust out of the enclosure.
  • Do not blow heat directly on the slab. It dries the surface too fast and can crack it. Heat the air under a tarp or enclosure instead, and keep a thermometer inside.

When you ask a rental company for a heater, tell them the area size, the temperature you want to hold and the fuel you can use. They can recommend the size.

Insulated blankets

For small slabs, patios and walkways, insulated curing blankets are often enough. Put them on as soon as the surface can take them without marking it. Pay attention to edges and corners, because they lose heat and freeze first.

Finishing in the cold

  • Wait for the bleed water to disappear before finishing. In the cold this takes longer, sometimes hours.
  • Do not sprinkle water or dry cement to speed things up.
  • Do not seal or use steel trowels too early. It can trap water and cause blisters or scaling.

Real-life example: a 10 x 10 ft patio in November

A 4 in thick slab of 10 x 10 ft needs about 1.23 cubic yards, or around 1.36 yards with 10% extra. Say the forecast is 38°F during the day and 28°F at night.

  1. The day before, lay insulated blankets on the ground to thaw it.
  2. Order ready-mix for the morning and tell the plant it is a cold-weather pour.
  3. Pour, screed and finish when the bleed water has gone.
  4. Cover the slab with blankets right away and add plastic over them. Keep it covered at night.
  5. Check the temperature under the cover. Keep protection on for at least the first few days and avoid heavy loads for longer than you would in summer.

Bagged concrete in cold weather

Store bags indoors so they are not frozen, mix with lukewarm (not hot) water, and mix small batches you can place right away. The covering and ground rules are the same as for a truck pour.

When to postpone

Consider waiting if the ground is frozen, if temperatures will stay well below freezing for several days after the pour, or if you cannot protect the slab for at least the first few days. A delayed pour costs less than ripping out a damaged slab.

After the pour

Cold slabs gain strength more slowly, so wait longer before heavy loads and sealing. See how long concrete takes to dry and cure and how much concrete a patio needs. Avoid de-icing salts during the first winter.

Frequently asked questions

What is the lowest temperature you can pour concrete?

There is no single cutoff. Pouring is possible below freezing if the mix, ground and protection are planned for it, but many homeowners should wait when freezing temperatures are expected in the first 48 hours. Below about 40°F (5°C) you should use cold-weather methods.

Does concrete cure faster with a heater?

A heater helps keep concrete warm, so it keeps gaining strength at a normal pace. It does not make concrete stronger than its design. Use vented heaters and avoid pointing heat directly at the surface.

Can I pour concrete on frozen ground?

No. Frozen subgrade can thaw and settle later, which causes cracks and sinking. Thaw the ground first, for example under insulated blankets, and remove snow and ice from forms.

How long should I keep a winter slab covered?

Commonly for at least the first few days and often up to a week, depending on temperature and mix. Keep it protected from freezing and keep it from drying out too fast. Follow your supplier's advice.

Should I use salt on a new concrete driveway in winter?

Avoid de-icing salts on a first-winter slab. Use sand for traction instead, and let the concrete cure for the recommended time before sealing.
